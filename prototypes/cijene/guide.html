Pricing · guide

Prices on the site, without boxing yourself in

Every amount here and in the five variants is a placeholder. The point is which kinds of numbers to publish and how, so that visitors and AI tools get something concrete while you keep room to price each job properly.

  1. Publish the shape of your prices, not quotes.Three kinds of number are safe to publish: fixed prices for small, repeatable things; a floor ("od 800 €") for each kind of build; and a typical range ("većina projekata 2.000–8.000 €"). None of them is a quote. The quote is always the written offer.
  2. Price the first step, not the whole project.Your process already contains a natural product: step 02, Idejno rješenje. Make it a small fixed-price step that is credited against the build. Then you never have to price a build up front, because the paid step is what produces the fixed quote.
  3. Charge bigger clients more through what they get.Scope, support level, urgency and value. Never through a secret rate for each client.
  4. Use the same numbers everywhere, in plain text.Use them on the site, in the meta description, in structured data, on Google Business Profile and LinkedIn, with currency, VAT status and a date.
  5. Start with variants 01 and 02 together.Details are at the end of the guide.

Why a published price doesn't box you in

Each kind of number commits you to something different. The trick is to publish only the kinds that commit you to little, and to make fixed promises only where the work really is the same every time.

The one risk of a floor, and the fix

People anchor on the first number they see. A bare "od 800 €" makes a 4.000 € offer sound expensive. Always pair the floor with the typical range, so the floor reads as "the smallest job" and not as "the price". Every variant does this.

What not to publish

  • One price for "a custom app". It's always wrong for someone, and it becomes the number everyone quotes back at you.
  • An hourly rate for builds. You build with AI, so you're fast, and hourly pricing punishes you for that. Bill outcomes (a fixed price per build) and keep the hourly rate for consulting and small changes, where time really is the product.
  • Discounts or promises you can't keep for every client. "Gotovo za 2 tjedna" on the page becomes a promise to everyone.

Charging different clients differently, the honest way

You said you'd like to charge a client who can pay more, more, even for similar work. Published prices don't stop that, as long as the difference lies in what they get. Bigger clients almost always need more of the things below, and listing them publicly (variant 02 does) makes the higher quote easy to explain.

  • Scope drivers. The number of users and locations, the number of programs it connects to, data migration, permissions and audit history, documentation. A 40-person company has more of all of them.
  • Support level. Response time, included hours and priority. Bigger clients pay for certainty: "isti radni dan" costs more than "unutar 2 radna dana" (variant 04).
  • Value. A tool that saves a three-person bakery 5 hours a week is worth less than the same tool saving a wholesaler 60 hours a week. Price the outcome and show the payback (variant 05 does this on the page; do it in every offer).
  • Three options in every written offer. A lean version, the recommended one and a fuller one. The client decides how much to spend. You never negotiate the price down, you only remove scope.
  • Urgency. A rush fee (say +25–50 %) for jobs that push other work aside.
  • Publish the standard price and discount openly, rather than publishing low and trying to charge more later. For example: "Obrti i mikro tvrtke: −15 % na prvi projekt." Giving a discount is easy. Explaining why your number is higher than the website's is not.

The one rule: same deliverable, same scope, same support → same price. Clients talk to each other, especially in a city the size of Rijeka.

Picking the actual numbers

  • Hourly rate (consulting). Divide your target yearly revenue by the hours you can really bill. A solo consultant rarely bills more than 800–1.000 hours a year once sales, admin and learning are counted. For example, 70.000 € ÷ 900 h ≈ 78 €, so 80 € / sat. Use round numbers.
  • Idejno rješenje (paid discovery). Roughly one or two days of your time, credited in full if they go ahead within 30–60 days. Agencies often use about 5–10 % of a typical project, which gives 200–400 € for a 2.000–8.000 € range. Keep the first 30-minute call free.
  • Build floor. The smallest job you'd still be glad to take, including discovery, the build and a month of support. You don't want work below that anyway, so the floor also filters enquiries.
  • Typical range. The middle ~70 % of your realistic projects. Until you have history, take the three most likely jobs in your pipeline and price them honestly.
  • Hosting. Make the cheapest plan clearly cheap, around 3–5× your real monthly cost per client, and put the value in the support levels above it.
  • Raise until some people say no. If nobody ever says "preskupo", you're too cheap. Review the prices every six months and date them on the page ("Vrijede od …").
  • Keep a price log. Record every offer, the amount and whether it was accepted. After 10–15 offers you'll know your real range, and you can replace the placeholder numbers with true ones.

Paid discovery, a caveat. Some small business owners won't want to pay for "a sketch". Keep it small, credit it fully, and for tiny jobs (a single automation under ~1.500 €) skip it and quote straight after the free call.

What AI tools and search engines need

An honest caveat first. I found no solid study showing that published prices make AI tools cite you more often. What exists is vendor research: Leapd reports that pricing pages and case studies bring more AI-referred traffic than generic guides, and Lightsite reports that companies' own product and service pages are now the largest group of sources ChatGPT and Perplexity cite. The practical argument is simpler. When someone asks an AI "koliko košta aplikacija po mjeri za mali obrt" or "tko mi može napraviti X do 2.000 €", it can only mention you if your page states numbers it can quote.

  • Prices as plain HTML text, not in images and not only produced by JavaScript. The variants keep every price in the HTML, including the hidden process cards in 01 and the rules table under the estimator in 05.
  • Questions phrased the way people ask them, each answered directly in its first sentence: "Koliko košta…? Od 800 €…" (variant 02).
  • Context: currency, VAT status ("bez PDV-a", or that you aren't in the VAT system, whichever is true), what's included, and the date.
  • The same numbers everywhere: on the site, Google Business Profile, LinkedIn and any directory listing. Conflicting numbers are worse than none.
  • Structured data. Each variant adds an OfferCatalog (with minPrice, price and unitCode) to the Organization in JSON-LD. When integrating, add hasOfferCatalog to the existing Organization node in pages/layout.templ, using only prices that are visible on the page, as the README already requires.
  • The meta description. The variants put the three headline prices in it: "Konzultacije 80 € po satu, izrada od 800 €, hosting od 30 € mjesečno."

What I would do

Recommendation

Combine 01 and 02. Keep the prices from 01 in the hero service cards and the process steps, so a visitor sees a number within the first scroll without anything looking like a price list. Then add the compact Cijene section from 02, with its FAQ, before "O meni" as the one place that states everything. Visitors see a number early, AI tools get plain-text answers and markup, and for builds you're committed only to floors and a range.

The business change matters more than the design: make Idejno rješenje a paid, fixed-price step that is credited against the build. It answers "I can't price a build up front": you don't have to, because you price the step that produces the quote.

Later: once you have 3–5 projects you can show, add priced examples in the style of 03, using real jobs. Borrow the hosting levels from 04 once you host more than a couple of clients. Build the estimator from 05 only after its rules have held up on real offers.

Before anything goes live

  • Set the consulting hourly rate and the price of the 5-hour package.
  • Decide whether Idejno rješenje is paid, what it costs, and the credit rule (for example, credited if you go ahead within 60 days).
  • Choose the build floor(s) and the typical range.
  • Define the cheapest hosting plan and exactly what it includes.
  • Settle the VAT wording.
  • Put a "valid from" date next to the prices.
  • Make the meta description and JSON-LD match the visible numbers exactly.
  • Use the same numbers on Google Business Profile and LinkedIn.
